# General visual-language space

For a general objective, the governing question is what the artifact has to accomplish for its
stated audience and setting, and what it should make them perceive while doing so. Neither
communication strategy nor perceptual experiment is assumed: the brief decides the balance, and the
six dimensions below are where that decision becomes visible.

Choose the visual language along the six dimensions below and state observable decisions rather than
family names.

Combine dimensions under the rules in the style-space index: one primary language, at most two
supporting techniques, contradictions resolved rather than averaged.

## 1. Medium and rendering logic

What physically makes the surface. This dimension is primary: it fixes what the other dimensions
may do.

- photographic capture: a lens produced the image; depth, focus, and exposure behave optically;
- lens-free rendering: three-dimensional geometry shaded by material response rather than by light
  recorded from a scene;
- drawn and painted: a wet or dry mark-making tool produced the surface, with the mark legible as
  such;
- printed reproduction: the image is built from a screening or impression process, and its artifacts
  are visible;
- assembled surfaces: cut, torn, pasted, stitched, or layered material joins are visible;
- vector construction: shapes are built from paths and fills, with no simulated physical surface;
- a hybrid where one medium visibly dominates and the other is applied in a bounded region.

Decide: which medium governs, and whether any second medium is present at all.

## 2. Composition and hierarchy

How the elements are arranged and in what order the eye meets them.

- one dominant element, with everything else subordinated by scale or isolation;
- a stated two-level jump, where the first reading and the second reading cannot be confused;
- a reading sequence fixed by numbered steps, panels, or a directional flow;
- a contrast pair, where the message is the difference between two visible states;
- a visible grid holding many parts in equal status, so the structure itself is read;
- an asymmetric balance where one heavy mass is answered by open space;
- a declared resting zone, so the eye has somewhere to stop before the next action.

Decide: what the eye meets first, second, and third, and the visual means that produce that order.
State the means — scale, isolation, contrast, position, sharpness — not "clear hierarchy".

## 3. Palette and contrast

Which colours are present, how many, and what they are doing.

- one hue carried by value steps alone;
- a near-neutral field with one saturated accent reserved for the decisive element;
- two complementary families held at unequal strength;
- a broad scheme held together by an even value structure;
- a printed-ink palette described as process behaviour rather than as fixed colour values;
- a monochrome scheme carried by material and light rather than by colour.

Decide: the palette's size, its dominant relationship, and where the highest contrast is spent.

## 4. Type and copy placement

How the approved wording is set, and where it sits in the layout.

- display type as the primary image, with the composition built around the setting;
- one text block in a reserved zone, with everything else kept out of it;
- copy integrated into the subject as a surface, label, or signage;
- a scale jump between headline and secondary text, with the middle size removed;
- a single line treated as a rule or as an edge of the layout;
- type kept small and dense, so the image carries the message and the copy confirms it.

Decide: the number of type sizes, the alignment logic, and the clear space the copy holds.

## 5. Material, light, and surface

What the image appears to be made of, and how it is lit.

- flat unmodulated colour with no simulated surface;
- paper, board, or fabric with visible fibre and edge behaviour;
- matte surfaces under diffuse light, with contact shadows doing the modelling;
- hard directional light that separates planes and states its source;
- emission or screen light, with the surface behaving as a display;
- worn, handled material, where use is visible and deliberate;
- reproduction artifacts used consciously: misregistration, halftone, compression.

Decide: the material claim and the light logic, and keep both consistent with the medium.

## 6. Density, rhythm, and address

How much is present, how fast it moves, and whom it speaks to.

- sparse: few elements, wide margins, slow reading, and a claim that can afford silence;
- dense: many elements, small type, tight intervals, and a reader expected to scan;
- metrical: an even beat that supports a repeated or ritual address;
- syncopated: an uneven beat that interrupts habit and draws attention to a specific element;
- declarative: large, flat, frontal, and addressed outward;
- intimate: small scale, close framing, and a voice addressed to one reader;
- instructional: numbered, aligned, and ordered for use rather than for atmosphere.

Decide: the density and the beat, and match them to the intended action.

## Reading the dimensions as a whole

Re-read the six decisions as one artifact. Confirm that the medium, the composition, the palette,
the type, and the light serve the same stated purpose, and that the material does not undercut it.
Name the intended reading in one sentence; if the decisions do not produce that sentence, one of
them is wrong.
