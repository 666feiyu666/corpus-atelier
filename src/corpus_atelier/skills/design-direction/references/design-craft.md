# Design craft checklist

Cross-cutting decisions that separate a resolved direction from a mood board. Adapted from the
prompt-craft notes published with GPT-Image2-Skill
(https://github.com/wuyoscar/GPT-Image2-Skill, MIT) and rewritten as design-stage guidance rather
than prompt syntax.

Use this as a diagnostic list. Apply only the items the brief actually depends on; do not turn
every item into a requirement.

## Canvas and reading distance first

Fix the deliverable, ratio, orientation, and where it will be seen before choosing a subject or a
style. A poster read at three metres, a feed thumbnail, and a printed A2 sheet impose different
hierarchies. State the layout zones before surface detail.

## Hierarchy is a reading path

Name what the eye meets first, second, and third, and the visual means that produce that order:
scale, placement, contrast, isolation, or sharpness. If two elements compete, the direction is
unresolved. Equal weight everywhere is not balance; it is a flat banner.

## Concrete decisions beat adjectives

Replace elegant, premium, modern, or dynamic with the decision that produces it: a specific
material, a light direction, a palette limit, a typographic jump, a crop. Six concrete nouns
describe more than a dozen quality words.

## Separate the controls

Material, lighting, palette, and level of realism are independent statements. Compressing them
into one mood word makes the direction untestable and invites contradictory renders. State the
light direction once and keep every element accountable to it.

## Style anchors stay specific and bounded

Choose one visual language and the two or three decisions that make it recognizable. Do not stack
compatible-sounding labels. A historical name is vocabulary, not a direction: use one only when its
principles already describe the decision you made, and never as a substitute for that decision.

## Text is a structural element

Treat approved copy as the exhaustive list of readable wording. Decide where each string sits, at
what scale, and in which reading order. Dense Chinese, bilingual, or small-print layouts need an
explicit structure, not smaller type. Decorative pseudo-text is never a substitute for copy.

## Negation only for strong priors

One or two targeted constraints against a plausible misreading of this specific brief. A long list
of "no ..." items is not a quality system, and it does not repair an unresolved positive
description.

## Multi-panel and repeated elements

Fix panel count and reading order. For grids, state row and column counts and keep axes, labels,
palette, and light identical across panels. Coherence across panels is the hard part, not the
number of panels.

## Editing an existing image

When a reference image is involved, the direction must name the single change and the invariants:
identity, framing, light, background, existing text, material character. A direction that
restates the unchanged content invites the renderer to redraw it.

## The three-glance test for graphic work

- First glance: the silhouette or theme is immediately recognizable.
- Second glance: the message, product promise, or narrative is legible.
- Third glance: close inspection is rewarded by texture, small labels, or detail.

If the second glance fails, no amount of third-glance detail helps.

## Category before style

Use the category guide for the deliverable to know what this artifact must get right. Style is
chosen to serve those requirements, never the reverse. When the brief names no style, a resolved
contemporary voice is a valid direction.
